DexFormer: Cross-Embodied Dexterous Manipulation via History-Conditioned Transformer

DexFormer presents a history-conditioned transformer policy that learns from procedurally generated dexterous-hand embodiments and transfers zero-shot to new hands for cross-embodiment manipulation.

TL;DR

DexFormer learns heterogeneous robot hand control using a history-conditioned Transformer without morphology identifiers or per-embodiment decoders. It is trained on 300 procedurally generated hand variants and validated for zero-shot transfer to LEAP, Allegro, and Rapid canonical hands. The average success rate is 77.50%, outperforming LSTM (58.72%) and GRU (44.29%), and it performs grasps in the real world with a distilled policy on the LEAP hand.

Problem Definition

This paper addresses the problem of performing dexterous grasping control with a single policy πθ\pi_\thetaπθ​ in situations where robot hand embodiments eee with different degrees of freedom (DoF), link lengths, masses, and inertias are not observed. In each episode, an object o∈Oo \in \mathcal{O}o∈O and an embodiment e∈Ee \in \mathcal{E}e∈E are sampled, and the policy must operate in a morphology-agnostic manner. The goal is the following RL objective:

max⁡θEe∼p(E),o∼p(O),τ∼πθ[∑t=0TγtR(ot,at)]\max _ {\theta} \mathbb {E} _ {e \sim p (\mathcal {E}), o \sim p (\mathcal {O}), \tau \sim \pi_ {\theta}} \left[ \sum_ {t = 0} ^ {T} \gamma^ {t} R (o _ {t}, a _ {t}) \right]θmax​Ee∼p(E),o∼p(O),τ∼πθ​​[t=0∑T​γtR(ot​,at​)]

Here, the embodiment eee is not explicitly provided as input to the policy. Therefore, the policy must handle the hand’s morphology and dynamics through learned representations rather than separate identifiers or per-embodiment decoder heads.

Core Idea

The core intuition is to allow the policy to infer the hand’s morphology and dynamics from recent observation-action history, rather than providing them as separate inputs. By aligning joints with the same anatomical function using canonical indices, hands with different DoFs can be mapped to a single action space. The Transformer then reads past tokens via causal attention to generate the current action distribution. Thanks to this structure, the learned policy can be applied zero-shot not only to canonical hands but also to unseen morphology variants.

Morphology Diversity Generation

Training embodiments are generated by sampling the morphology parameter z(e)z(e)z(e) of the canonical hand from a perturbation distribution pmorphp_{\text{morph}}pmorph​. This process varies link lengths, masses, and inertias while maintaining the kinematic graph topology and actuation structure. 100 training variants are generated for each canonical LEAP, Allegro, and RAPID hand, totaling 300 embodiments used for training. For testing, 32 unseen variants per hand sampled from the same distribution are used, while canonical hands are excluded from training and evaluated zero-shot.

Training and Deployment

Training is performed in Isaac Lab, with key results reported using 4096 parallel environments and 40k episodes. Automatic Domain Randomization (ADR) is used to gradually increase noise levels as policy performance improves. Randomization targets include joint positions, joint velocities, fingertip states, point-cloud observations, and gravity. In distributed training, each GPU computes local rollouts and forward passes, and gradients are averaged via all-reduce during the backpropagation step to synchronize weights across all devices.

Offline distillation is used for real-world deployment. 2000 episodes of expert demonstrations are recorded, and a student MLP with an observation dimension of 158 is trained. The student learning rate is 0.001, and the optimizer is Adam. In real-world control, the arm joint-space controller operates at 1000 Hz, while policy evaluation runs at 10 Hz.

In the history length ablation, 5-step was generally better than 1-step. The overall average was 73.00% for 1-step and 77.50% for 5-step. However, cases were observed where 5-step performed lower than 1-step for RAPID. For RAPID canonical, it decreased from 77.59% to 71.69%, and for variants, it decreased from 82.75% to 77.09%.

In the training embodiment diversity ablation, with a setting of 25k episodes, 100 hands performed best. The overall averages were 80.18% for 25 hands, 79.34% for 50 hands, and 84.09% for 100 hands. This demonstrates that the diversity of morphology randomization is important for generalization.

In the parallel environments scalability ablation, the number of parallel environments was increased with a setting of 30k episodes and 3 GPUs. The canonical averages were 69.24% for 1024/GPU, 74.41% for 2048/GPU, and 78.07% for 4096/GPU, while the variant averages were 70.84%, 75.58%, and 81.21%, respectively. However, instability was observed where RAPID canonical decreased from 74.34% to 69.72% at 4096/GPU.

In the joint locking experiment, locking distal flexion joints and low-leverage MCP abduction joints showed relatively minor performance degradation. In contrast, locking basal flexion and thumb CMC joints caused significant performance drops. All impaired variants performed lower than or equal to canonical zero-shot performance, and a 4-second execution window showed higher success rates than a 2-second window, indicating some compensatory effect.

Limitations

  • Limited object-level generalization due to insufficient diversity in object geometry and mass.
  • Zero-shot performance degradation is observed with high-DoF embodiments like RAPID.
  • Due to Isaac Lab constraints, embodiments with different kinematic topologies must be trained on separate GPUs, and mixed buffers or centralized gradient accumulation are not supported.

Critical Review

Strengths

  • Attempts cross-embodiment zero-shot transfer using only history without morphology identifiers — DexFormer is differentiated from existing single-embodiment policies by inferring morphology and dynamics in real-time using a history-conditioned Transformer conditioned on past observation-action pairs, without using explicit morphology identifiers or per-embodiment decoder heads (Evidence: Section 1, Fig. 3).
  • Relatively systematic baseline comparisons and component ablations — Presents results in tables for LSTM/GRU baselines along with history length, training embodiment diversity, and parallel environment scale, and verifies joint locking robustness (Evidence: Table II–V, Fig. 7).
  • Includes both simulation zero-shot transfer and real-world distillation evaluation — Shows qualitative/quantitative results including unseen variant evaluations on procedurally generated morphology variants, as well as per-object success rates and DoF-reduced variants after offline distillation in a Franka+LEAP real-world setup (Evidence: Section 4, Section 6, Appendix B, Table VI, Fig. 13–15).
  • Explicitly defines shared action space and reward design — Combines functional role-based canonical finger action space atFa_t^FatF​ and arm action atAa_t^AatA​, and organizes approach/grasp/pose tracking rewards in formulas in Table I, enhancing the readability of the method (Evidence: Section 2, Table I, Fig. 2).

Weaknesses & Questions

  • Lacks direct comparison with recent cross-embodiment baselines — The provided experiments only compare against 3-layer LSTM/GRU, lacking quantitative comparisons with cross-embodiment dexterous grasping methods like GET-Zero, CeDex, and D(R,O)Grasp mentioned in the references (Evidence: Section 4 Baseline, References [15], [23], [24]) (Interpretation).
  • Repetition, variance, and statistical significance of success rates are not reported — Tables II–VI present only single success rate values, lacking standard deviations per seed, confidence intervals, or paired tests, making it difficult to judge the stability of advantages like the 77.50% average (Evidence: Table II–V, Table VI) (Interpretation).
  • Real-world performance is low and variant results are qualitative — Real-world per-object success rates are low (1/5 to 4/5), and DoF-reduced LEAP hand variants only have qualitative images in Fig. 15 without numerical success rates or execution times (Evidence: Appendix B, Table VI, Fig. 15) (Interpretation).
  • Increasing history length actually degrades performance for RAPID — In Table III, RAPID canonical reverses from 77.59% for 1-step to 71.69% for 5-step, and variants reverse from 82.75% to 77.09%. While the overall average favors 5-step, the benefit of history conditioning is inconsistent for high-DoF embodiments (Evidence: Table III) (Interpretation).
  • Cross-embodiment generalization scope is limited to the same kinematic topology family — Training embodiments are morphology variants perturbing link length/mass/inertia of canonical hands, and the limitations section explicitly states that embodiments with different topologies must be trained on separate GPUs due to Isaac Lab constraints. Therefore, the “cross-embodiment” claim reads as a narrower scope than morphology randomization generalization (Evidence: Section 2 Embodiment Generation, Section 8 Limitations) (Interpretation).
  • Reward design is strongly coupled with success metrics, limiting task diversity verification — rTr^TrT and rSr^SrS directly reward target pose tracking, and success is defined as ∥pgoal−ptarget∥2<0.05\|p^{goal}-p^{target}\|_2<0.05∥pgoal−ptarget∥2​<0.05, which can be interpreted as performance dependent on reward shaping (Evidence: Section 3, Table I, Section 4 Success Metric) (Interpretation).

Reproducibility

  • The provided paper summary does not confirm code/data availability, and details such as Transformer hidden size/head count, optimizer/learning rate, pmorphp_{\text{morph}}pmorph​ perturbation ranges, automatic domain randomization (ADR) schedule, reward parameter tuning, simulation point cloud generation parameters, real-world distillation student MLP structure/loss, wall-clock training time, and random seeds with variance reporting are missing (Evidence: Section 4, Section 6, Appendix A–B).
