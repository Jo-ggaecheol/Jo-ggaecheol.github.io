La-JEPA: Compact Robot Policies with Latent World Modeling

La-JEPA is a compact world-action model that trains its policy from scratch in the feature space of a pretrained V-JEPA 2.1 encoder, jointly predicting future features during training and generating only actions at inference.

TL;DR

  • To reduce the large Transformer backbones and high inference costs inherited by existing generative World-Action Models (WAMs) and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies, we propose a compact WAM trained from scratch on top of V-JEPA 2.1 latent features.
  • World DiT constructs latent world representations using observations and future features, while Action DiT denoises action chunks via flow matching. Future generation is omitted during inference.
  • On LIBERO-Plus with corrected instructions, La-JEPA L achieves 88.0%, outperforming NowWAM (86.2%). On RoboTwin 2.0, the clean-trained B model averages 60.8%, and the physical policy call latency is 25.16ms for B, which is shorter than Fast-WAM’s 88.09ms.

Problem Definition

Existing WAMs and VLA policies often directly inherit Transformer architectures designed for video generation or large-scale language-vision modeling for robot control. While this structure offers high expressiveness, it can lead to increased inference costs and an unclear relationship with actual control performance. In particular, most parameters in the generative backbone may not be directly necessary for robot action prediction, and including frozen encoders causes the number of deployment parameters to grow rapidly.

La-JEPA frames this problem as the question: “Can we achieve sufficient control performance with a compact policy trained from scratch in a pre-trained latent feature space, without using a generative backbone?” The goal is to achieve high success rates with few trainable parameters, maintain relative robustness against distribution shifts such as camera changes or sensor noise, and keep low latency on physical robots.

Core Idea

The intuition behind La-JEPA is to use observation features and future target features created by V-JEPA 2.1 as control representations, rather than generating RGB videos. World DiT processes current observation tokens and future feature tokens together to form a latent world representation, while Action DiT is conditioned on this representation to denoise only the action chunks. Attention masks prevent action tokens from directly accessing future tokens, allowing the World DiT’s representation to be shaped by future prediction supervision during training, while eliminating future generation costs during inference. This design shares the “world model that predicts the future” and the “policy that outputs actions” within the same latent space, but retains only action decoding at the deployment stage.

Methodology

Observation Encoding and Conditional Sequences

Visual inputs utilize observation features zctxz_{ctx}zctx​ and future target features zfutz_{fut}zfut​ processed by the V-JEPA 2.1 ViT-L encoder. There is no RGB video generation path, and policy learning occurs in this latent feature space. Language instructions and proprioceptive states are transformed into conditional sequences zcondz_{cond}zcond​ via a frozen FLAN-T5-large and learnable projections.

Spatial token reduction varies by benchmark. The final recipe uses patch grouping for RoboTwin 2.0 and 2x mean pooling for the LIBERO suite. In settings that use only stored features, the entire demonstration is pre-encoded, and the encoder is not run during training. Conversely, in settings using image augmentation and encoder LoRA, current observation clips are encoded online, while future targets use values stored by a frozen encoder.

World DiT and Action DiT

La-JEPA trains two blocks, World DiT and Action DiT, from scratch. World DiT processes observation tokens and future feature tokens, while Action DiT processes action chunks. In the attention mask, action tokens can only access observation and action tokens, not future tokens directly. Consequently, action prediction indirectly utilizes future information through the latent representation formed by World DiT.

This structure performs both future feature prediction and action prediction during training but allows for the omission of future token generation during inference. Model sizes are provided in three variants: S, B, and L. The experiments section summarizes trainable and deployment parameters.

Training Objective

La-JEPA uses flow matching as its training objective. It samples time τf\tau_fτf​, τa\tau_aτa​ and noise ϵf\epsilon_fϵf​, ϵa\epsilon_aϵa​ in the future feature and action spaces, respectively, and the model predicts the corresponding velocities v^f\hat{v}_fv^f​, v^a\hat{v}_av^a​. The loss is defined as the mean squared error of the two velocity predictions.

Here, vfv_fvf​ and vav_ava​ are the flow-matching velocity targets for future features and actions, respectively. This loss encourages World DiT’s latent representation to be useful for predicting future observations and guides Action DiT to generate executable action chunks from that representation.

Inference Procedure

During inference, future tokens are removed. Action chunks are initialized with Gaussian noise and denoised by iterating Euler integration. During this process, the key/value cache of World DiT is reused to reduce action decoding costs. The policy predicts 32 actions per call, and the number of actions executed per replanning cycle varies by benchmark. LIBERO uses a setting where 10 actions are executed per replan, while RoboTwin 2.0 executes 24 actions.

Final Recipe and Training Settings

The final recipe includes several design choices. It uses predictive supervision (performing future feature prediction alongside action prediction) compared to an action-prediction-only baseline, and employs the V-JEPA 2.1 feature space instead of Wan2.2 VAE latents. For time encoding, tube-motion is selected among static, tube, and tube-motion. To improve robustness, camera dropout and proprioceptive dropout are applied with a probability of 0.1, and image augmentation and encoder LoRA are used together.

Training settings use the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 5×10−45 \times 10^{-4}5×10−4, betas (0.9,0.95)(0.9, 0.95)(0.9,0.95), and matrix weight decay of 0.01. The schedule consists of 150k updates, 5% warmup, cosine decay, and a learning rate floor fixed at 1% in the last 10% interval. Batch sizes are 128 for LIBERO and 400 for RoboTwin, using BF16 and EMA decay of 0.9999. Actions and states are normalized to the [−1,1][-1, 1][−1,1] range via per-channel percentile normalization, with RoboTwin clamped at ±5\pm 5±5. Features are normalized using training set statistics.

Experimental Results

Evaluation Protocol and Instruction Correction

Experiments are conducted on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, RoboTwin 2.0, and physical robot tasks based on the Franka Panda. Re-evaluation targets for public checkpoints include StarVLA, JEPA-WAM, Fast-WAM, Cosmos Policy, ImageWAM, NowWAM, LeapBot-WA, π0\pi_0π0​, and π0.5\pi_{0.5}π0.5​-JEPA-WAM. Faster-WAM is a baseline self-trained with clean demonstrations. Qwen-RobotManip and RoboTwin 2.0 leaderboard methods are compared using reported citation values.

In LIBERO-Plus, the instruction issue in public evaluators is significant. Public evaluators may induce instructions from modified filenames, potentially providing text mixed with parameters not seen during training. This paper proposes a corrected evaluation that uses the original training instruction of the base task for non-linguistic variations. With this correction, Fast-WAM increased by +19.3pp, LeapBot-WA by +8.1pp, and La-JEPA S/B/L by +6.3/+4.9/+3.9pp respectively, while NowWAM remained unchanged.

Limitations

  • The authors state that they did not perform a systematic search of hyperparameters such as learning rate and dropout rates.
  • They did not verify the scaling effects of embodied pretraining.
  • They acknowledge lower performance than embodied pretraining models in Randomized RoboTwin.

Critical Review

Strengths

  • Systematically designed component-wise ablations — Performance contributions were quantified by isolating predictive supervision, feature space (V-JEPA vs Wan2.2), time encoding (Static/Tube/Tube-Motion), token reduction (Mean Pooling vs Patch Grouping), dropout, and augmentation+LoRA as independent variables (Evidence: Appendix A full text, Fig 3, Table 6). Specifically, predictive supervision reports a 3-seed comparison and p-value (p<10−3p < 10^{-3}p<10−3) on LIBERO-Plus, partially mitigating the chance of single-experiment anomalies (Evidence: Appendix A.1, Table 7).
  • Inference efficiency backed by measurements — Latency for the entire policy call (tokenization to decoding) was measured on a consumer GPU, showing La-JEPA B is approximately 3.5x faster than Fast-WAM (25.16ms vs 88.09ms). Physical robot experiments also confirmed that completion times are equivalent or superior to competing models (Evidence: Appendix C Table 15, Appendix D Table 17).
  • Discovery of benchmark evaluator flaws and proposal of a correction protocol — Identified the issue where LIBERO-Plus public evaluators include parameters not seen during training in instructions, corrupting sentences. Applied a correction using base task instructions for non-linguistic variations. This correction showed changes in existing rankings (e.g., Fast-WAM +19.3pt, LeapBot-WA +8.1pt), contributing to improved benchmark reliability (Evidence: Table 4, Table 13, Table 14).

Weaknesses & Questions

  • Clear generalization limits in Randomized RoboTwin — Performance drops to less than half from 86.2% Clean to 35.4% Randomized, and the authors acknowledge it is lower than Embodied PT models (Evidence: Table 2, Limitations section). This conflicts with the claim that V-JEPA feature space is robust to visual variations, and the conclusion that “compact policies are more accurate than larger generative-based models” appears limited to Clean conditions (Interpretation).
  • Most ablations lack statistical evidence due to single runs — Appendix A explicitly states “single run results (exception: LIBERO-Plus 3-seed comparison for predictive supervision),” making it impossible to determine if core design decisions like feature space comparison (+15.9pt), time encoding (+3.4pt), and dropout (+7.1pt) are significant within seed-to-seed variability (Evidence: Appendix A common settings).
  • Checkpoint selection criteria biased toward Clean performance optimization — Checkpoints are selected from a 1,000-episode Clean grid. Table 12 shows that while Clean improved by +5~8pt compared to the original (120k), Randomized decreased by -5~-9pt with the corrected (150k) version. This may hide the trade-off where Clean-optimized checkpoints sacrifice OOD performance (Evidence: Appendix B “Data and Checkpoint Selection”, Table 12).
  • Inconsistent baseline comparison conditions — JEPA-WAM is compared using a restricted set of 20 tasks, while La-JEPA was trained on all 50 tasks. Some baselines (StarVLA, Cosmos Policy, etc.) are re-evaluated public checkpoints, while others (Qwen-RobotManip, RoboTwin leaderboard) are cited from reports, meaning training data volume, initialization, and optimization conditions are not identical (Evidence: Appendix B “Benchmark Protocol”, “Baseline Sources”).
  • Low statistical power in physical robot experiments — With 100 demonstrations per task, 4 tasks, and 20 trials each, the difference between La-JEPA (17.50/20) and π0.5\pi_{0.5}π0.5​ (17.25/20) is only 0.25 trials, with no variance information to judge if this difference is significant (Evidence: Table 5, Appendix D).
  • Unclear practical benefits of Flow Matching — Reducing denoising steps from the default to 3 results in negligible success rate changes (L: 82.41% vs 82.26%), leaving it unconfirmed whether adopting flow matching provides actual efficiency advantages over diffusion (Evidence: Appendix E.2 Table 19).

Reproducibility

  • Hyperparameters (AdamW LR 5×10−45\times10^{-4}5×10−4, batch size, LoRA rank/α, augmentation probability/strength, normalization ranges) are detailed in Appendix B Tables 9·10, and MuJoCo version (3.3.2) and checkpoint selection protocols are specified, lowering the barrier to training reproduction. However, code/dataset availability is not mentioned anywhere in the text, and exact checkpoint versions for V-JEPA 2.1 encoder and FLAN-T5-large are not specified, making it uncertain if the same feature space can be reproduced (Interpretation).
