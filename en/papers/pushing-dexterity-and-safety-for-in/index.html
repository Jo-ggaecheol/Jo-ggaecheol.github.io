Pushing Dexterity and Safety for In-Hand Object Pose Reaching That Works Remarkably Well

We present a sim-to-real recipe for in-hand object pose reaching with a multi-fingered hand at arbitrary wrist orientations, transferring zero-shot to a real LEAP hand. A learned residual safety filter is added to greatly reduce drops while maintaining high goal-reaching performance.

TL;DR

To address the limitations of existing research—which is confined to a single wrist orientation and overlooks safety (drops)—we propose a method for zero-shot transfer of policies learned in simulation to a real LEAP hand. By introducing an adaptive friction curriculum and a ResSafe-based residual safety filter, we achieved a 95.2% goal success rate based on the state policy and reduced drops by 75% during 13.3 hours of real-robot evaluation.

Problem Definition

Most existing research on in-hand object pose reaching operates only with the palm facing up (a single wrist orientation) and is limited to simple rotations or repetitive motions. Furthermore, safety measures to prevent objects from falling out of the hand (drops) are often not explicitly addressed or are handled implicitly. This paper points out the need for a policy that can move an object to a desired position and orientation in any wrist orientation, along with a safety mechanism that minimizes drops while performing this task.

Core Idea

The core challenge is to reduce the simulation-to-real (Sim-to-Real) gap when applying policies learned without human data in simulation to real robots, while simultaneously addressing the safety issue of object drops. To achieve this, we propose two main components. First, we overcome physical limitations in simulation by using an Adaptive Friction Curriculum, which starts with unrealistically high friction coefficients and gradually reduces them to realistic values. Second, we introduce a ResSafe-based Safety Filter that adds a residual to the task policy’s actions to correct for safety. This filter allows for flexible management of the trade-off between performance and safety by adjusting the conservatism parameter www.

Methodology

Hardware and Simulation Environment

We use a LEAP hand (16 Dynamixel XC330 servos) mounted on a UR5 arm. High-friction 3M grip tape is attached to the fingertips, while the rest of the hand consists of low-friction plastic. Visual input is divided into two types: motion capture (OptiTrack) and a wrist-mounted stereo camera (Orbbec Gemini 305). The simulator uses MuJoCo Warp based on mjlab, and the actuator model is identified using pendulum test bench data via the BAM (Bio-inspired Actuator Model). This minimizes latency and dynamics differences with the real robot.

Learning Pipeline

Learning proceeds in two tracks: Task Policy and Safety Filter.

  1. Task Policy (A1-A4):
    • A1: Teacher PPO learning with privileged observations (noise-free state information).
    • A2: Distillation into a deployable state-based Student using DAgger.
    • A3: Fine-tuning the student policy with PPO.
    • A4: Distillation into a student that observes only the depth camera using DAgger.
  2. Safety Filter (B1-B2):
    • B1: Learning a ResSafe-based residual safety filter on top of the frozen task policy.
    • B2: Distilling the state-based filter into a depth-based filter.

Ablation

  • Friction Curriculum: Applying only the friction curriculum increased the number of goals per episode from 1.87 to 2.14. The combination of goal and friction curricula was most effective (2.27).
  • Privileged Observations: An actor with privileged observations is essential in stage A1. Using only noisy student observations reduced performance by half (2.27 vs 1.12 goals/ep).
  • Penalty Type: L2 penalty provided a better balance between goal success rate and drop rate compared to L1 penalty (Coarse success rate of 80.4% vs 21.7% at w=0.5w=0.5w=0.5).

Limitations

The authors explicitly state the following limitations:

  1. Current policies and filters must be learned per object. A single policy cannot handle multiple objects.
  2. There is a visual sim-to-real gap for the Knife and Wineglass. Specifically, for the depth policy, pose estimation errors on the real robot exhibited long tails compared to simulation, leading to performance degradation.
  3. We did not achieve a 100% success rate and zero drops.

Critical Review

Strengths

  • Large-scale and controlled real-robot evaluation — Evaluated on a held-out grasp dataset (not used for training) with 4 objects, 6 wrist orientations, 96 runs, and 4,800 unseen goals. The setup is designed so that if the object and wrist orientation are the same, all configurations attempt the same goal sequence, ensuring fair comparison between configurations (Evidence: Section VI-B, Appendix J).
  • Quantitative demonstration of safety filter performance/safety trade-off — The residual safety filter with w=0.5w=0.5w=0.5 reduced coarse success rate from 95.2% to 91.5% for the state policy while reducing drops from 16 to 4. A www sweep provides a trade-off curve between time to first drop and goal success rate (Evidence: Table II, Fig. 5, Table XI).
  • Isolated analysis of sim-to-real visual gap causes — An auxiliary object-pose head was attached to the depth policy and compared with motion capture. While errors were similar to simulation for the Rubber Duck and Lightbulb (median ~1 mm, within 1∘1^\circ1∘), long tails in real-world errors were observed only for the Knife and Wineglass (Evidence: Appendix K, Fig. 26).
  • Detailed actuator and latency compensation — Parameters for the voltage-driven DC motor model (BAM m5), firmware/encoder/power settings, and command channel latency are specified in Table III. Chirp input validation reports a joint RMSE of 0.45° between real and simulated data (Evidence: Appendix B, Table III, Fig. 15–16).

Weaknesses & Questions

  • HJ filter comparison limited to single object/policy — The direct evidence for ResSafe’s superiority, the comparison with Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) filters (LRSF, HCSF), was performed only on the Rubber Duck state policy. It is not reported for the other 3 objects or the depth policy (Evidence: Table I). Therefore, it remains unconfirmed whether ResSafe is superior to HJ filters in all configurations (Interpretation).
  • Training seed variability not reflected in real-robot results — The deployed task policies are single A1–A4 training runs per object, and some ablations (e.g., 4-object curriculum) used only one seed. The 96 real-robot experiments are repetitions of the same checkpoint and do not reflect training seed diversity (Evidence: Appendix H, Section VI-B).
  • Large sim-to-real gap for Knife/Wineglass in depth policy, not fully resolved by filter — Depth policy coarse success rates were 54% for Knife (90% in simulation) and 50% for Wineglass (84% in simulation). Wineglass drops only reduced from 51 to 40. The title’s claim that it “works remarkably well” may underestimate this limitation (Evidence: Section VI-B, Appendix K).
  • Safety filter degrades performance in multi-stage downstream tasks — At w=1.5w=1.5w=1.5, the success rate for the Rubber Duck place stage dropped from 7/10 to 3/10, and Lightbulb screwed-in from 4/5 to 3/5. The benefits of the filter are not guaranteed outside of pose reaching (Evidence: Table XIV).
  • Limited reporting of statistical significance/variance — Real-robot results are reported based on pooled 300 goals per object, without explicit p-values or confidence intervals. Standard error of the mean (s.e.m.) error bars are provided only in some simulation ablations (Fig. 28) (Evidence: Table II, Fig. 28).

Reproducibility

  • Code and checkpoints are noted as “coming soon.” The appendix provides detailed information on hardware, system identification, simulator settings, domain randomization, and training time/hyperparameters (Evidence: Note 8, Appendix A–H). However, limitations exist for full reproduction and extension due to the single training seed for deployed checkpoints, HJ filter comparison limited to the Rubber Duck, and lack of a proposed solution for the depth gap.
