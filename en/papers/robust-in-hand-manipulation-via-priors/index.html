Robust In-Hand Manipulation via Priors in Reinforcement Learning and Mechanical Design

The paper embeds two physics priors—a global grasp-quality prior from classical grasp analysis and a local contact-geometry prior from fingertip curvature—into reinforcement-learning reward shaping and anisotropic fingertip design for robust in-hand rolling with a multifingered hand. This improves rotation efficiency, grasp stability, disturbance rejection, and sim-to-real transfer under proprioception-only feedback.

TL;DR

  • We address the reinforcement learning problem of performing in-hand rotation using only proprioception, without external sensors.
  • We incorporate global grasp quality into reward shaping and anisotropic fingertip curvature into mechanical design as physics priors.
  • Adding aligned tips to the baseline (tips + GQ) improves the summary success rate from 56% to 83%, and increases cumulative cylinder rotation up to 2.29 turns.

Problem Definition

This paper addresses the problem of rotating objects in-hand using only proprioception, without external sensors such as tactile or visual inputs. Existing computation-algorithm-based methods rely on accurate system identification and contact modeling, making them difficult to extend to real-world uncertainties and high-degree-of-freedom hands. Conversely, existing reinforcement learning (RL) approaches are robust to rotation dynamics but may neglect stable grasp maintenance. Furthermore, sim-to-real transfer studies often rely on additional hardware like motion capture or tactile sensors, or focus on approximating simulation-only privileged information. To mitigate these limitations, this paper injects physics priors into two layers—learning rewards and mechanical design—to achieve robust in-hand manipulation across various hand orientations and object types.

Core Ideas

The intuition is to decouple “enabling good rotation” from “preventing grasp failure.” Global grasp quality, a classical grasp analysis metric computable solely from contact positions, is used for reward shaping. The wrench w=Gf\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{G}\mathbf{f}w=Gf generated by forces f\mathbf{f}f at KKK contact points is expressed using the Gramian matrix M=GG⊤\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^\topM=GG⊤, and its minimum eigenvalue is defined as:

qgrasp=λmin⁡(M)q_{\mathrm{grasp}}=\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{M})qgrasp​=λmin​(M)

A larger value indicates less vulnerability to specific wrench directions. The local contact geometry prior involves designing fingertip curvature to allow rolling in the working direction while suppressing off-axis motion. Specifically, aligned cylindrical tips have high relative curvature in the direction of rotating a cylindrical object but near-zero curvature in the vertical tangent direction that destabilizes the grasp.

Methodology

RMA-based Learning Pipeline

The experimental platform consists of a Franka Research 3 arm equipped with an Allegro Hand. The hand has 16 active degrees of freedom, and the policy outputs 16-dimensional target joint positions, which are tracked by a PD controller. Simulation is performed in Isaac Gym with parallel environments for learning; policy queries run at 20 Hz, control decimation is 6, and episode length is 400 steps.

Learning follows the RMA (Rapid Motor Adaptation) framework. In Stage I, a teacher policy learns via PPO using both proprioceptive observations ot\mathbf{o}_tot​ and latent variables zt\mathbf{z}_tzt​ derived from privileged information. Privileged information includes object pose, scale, mass, friction coefficients, and center-of-mass offsets. In Stage II, an adaptation module estimates z^t\hat{\mathbf{z}}_tz^t​ using only the last 30 frames of proprioceptive history, and a student policy generates actions from (ot,z^t)(\mathbf{o}_t,\hat{\mathbf{z}}_t)(ot​,z^t​). At deployment time, only proprioception is used, without privileged information.

Initial grasp caches are generated by filtering canonical pose variations. Configurations are retained if they maintain stable grasps with at least three fingertip contacts, proximity conditions to the object, and stability under a 90∘90^\circ90∘ rotation around the arm axis. Approximately 50,000 initial grasps are created per object scale. Learning proceeds with a hand orientation curriculum, gradually increasing palm tilt from 0∘0^\circ0∘, 45∘45^\circ45∘, 90∘90^\circ90∘, to 180∘180^\circ180∘ to expose the policy to gravity-induced perturbations. At each reset, object scale, mass, friction, center-of-mass offset, and PD gains are randomized, and short external forces are applied. In the real world, only post-hoc pose is measured using a RealSense D435 and AprilTags; this information is not used as policy input.

Reward Design

The reward consists of task terms, grasp quality shaping terms, and regularization terms. The task term decomposes object angular velocity into components parallel and perpendicular to the target rotation axis, rewarding desired directional rotation while suppressing off-axis rotation.

The grasp quality shaping term uses qgraspq_{\mathrm{grasp}}qgrasp​ calculated from contact positions in the hand coordinate frame, as defined earlier. It requires no force sensing and is converted into a bounded monotonically increasing reward through adaptive scaling and clipping. This term encourages the policy to maintain grasp configurations with high wrench resistance rather than simply rotating quickly.

The regularization term penalizes object displacement, deviation from the initial grasp cache, and torque/work. The total reward is defined as the sum of these three terms.

Fingertip Design and Fabrication

Fingertips are designed in four shapes: flat, spherical, aligned cylindrical, and orthogonal cylindrical. External dimensions, length, and mounting methods are identical; only the contact surface geometry differs. The radius of curvature is R=40R=40R=40 mm, i.e., κf=1/R=25 m−1\kappa_f=1/R=25\ \mathrm{m}^{-1}κf​=1/R=25 m−1.

In local contact geometry, relative curvature is viewed as K=Ko+KfK=K_o+K_fK=Ko​+Kf​. For a cylindrical object, the aligned cylindrical tip provides high relative curvature in the working direction (XcX_cXc​) to support rolling, while setting curvature to zero in the off-axis direction (YcY_cYc​) to passively suppress out-of-plane motion. Spherical and orthogonal shapes have curvature in the YcY_cYc​ direction as well, potentially allowing off-axis rolling.

Fabrication is performed via silicone casting using a reusable multi-part mold. After sanding and applying release agent coating to PLA 3D-printed molds, Smooth-On Dragon Skin 30 silicone is poured. Once cured and demolded, the fingertips feature integrated brass screw inserts for mounting to the distal joints of the Allegro Hand.

Experimental Results

Evaluation metrics include Rot (cumulative rotation), TTT (episode duration), and SR (success rate). Success is defined as a gated success rate requiring both maintaining the grasp for at least 10 seconds and achieving a minimum of 0.15 turns. Each condition was evaluated with 3 seeds and 3 rollouts.

In simulation ablations, the design combining both priors proved strongest.

This demonstrates that fingertip geometry itself creates favorable contacts for continuous rotation, even without policy retraining.

Limitations

The limitations explicitly stated by the authors in the paper are as follows:

  • Other task categories such as transport or regrasp were not evaluated.
  • Quantitative evaluation against direct external perturbations is lacking.
  • The contribution of each component to sim-to-real transfer could not be quantitatively isolated.

Critical Review

Strengths

  • Experimental structure allowing separation of the effects of two physics priors — Comparisons with/without Global Grasp Quality (GQ) reward and fingertip swaps under a fixed policy allow for separate evaluation of the contributions of “learning reward prior” and “mechanical geometry prior.” (Evidence: Table 1, Appendix Table 7; Table 2, Appendix Table 8; Table 9)
  • Physical geometric interpretation consistent with experimental results — Relative curvature and tangential mobility analysis predicts that aligned cylindrical fingertips allow rolling in the working direction while suppressing off-axis motion, which is supported by real-world results where aligned tips dominate in Success Rate (SR), Time To Termination (TTT), and Rotation (Rot). (Evidence: Sec 2.3, Table 4, Fig 6; Table 8, Table 9)
  • Proprioception-only zero-shot real-world evaluation and failure mode analysis — Cameras are used only for post-hoc pose measurement, palm tilt and object types are varied, and gated SR with termination decomposition excludes simple holding from success. (Evidence: Appendix C, Appendix E; Fig 12, Fig 13; Table 7–8)
  • Relatively detailed training/fabrication information required for reproduction — RMA (Rapid Motor Adaptation) stages, observation/latent variable structures, domain randomization ranges, reward functions, network and optimization hyperparameters, and silicone fingertip materials/dimensions are provided. (Evidence: Appendix C Table 5–6, Eq 12–16; Appendix D)

Weaknesses & Questions

  • Weak statistical basis for real-world repeated runs — Key real-world results are reported as averages over a small number of seeds and rollouts, and Tables 1 and 2 do not specify standard deviations, confidence intervals, or statistical significance tests, making it difficult to judge if improvements exceed noise. (Evidence: Sec 4 “average of 3 seeds, 3 rollouts”; Table 1, Table 2; Std only in Table 9)
  • No direct comparison of superiority over prior work — While related work is described, there is no same-condition comparison with external baselines such as tactile/visual-based methods or existing sim-to-real in-hand RL policies, limiting the strength of the claim that it is “more stable than existing RL approaches.” (Evidence: Appendix A; Sec 4 presents only internal GQ/tip ablations)
  • Limited evaluation scope requires caution in generalization conclusions — With only cylinders/cubes/spheres, Allegro Hand + Franka Research 3, fixed palm tilt poses, and no quantitative evaluation of regrasp/transport/external perturbations, it is difficult to broadly interpret the scope of “robust in-hand manipulation.” (Evidence: Sec 5–6 Limitations; Appendix E object list; Fig 5)
  • Sim-to-real transfer and RMA component contributions not quantitatively isolated — While zero-shot real-world success is reported, there are no ablations showing how performance changes with simple policy learning without Stage I/II, domain randomization, or privileged latent variable estimation, leaving the cause of transfer improvement unclear. (Evidence: Appendix C RMA/DR settings; mentioned as limitation in Sec 5–6)
  • Limited exploration of design space for local geometry prior — Only four fingertips are compared, and the impact of changes in radius of curvature, material hardness, or friction characteristics on the aligned effect is not evaluated, making it difficult to generalize whether “aligned cylindrical” is optimal. (Evidence: Sec 3.3; Appendix D; Table 2/8)
  • Insufficient verification of physical completeness and sensitivity of GQ prior — The grasp quality metric is defined by the minimum eigenvalue without considering the friction cone, and there are no sensitivity experiments for reward shaping parameters like adaptive scaling, EMA, or clipping, making it hard to know if the GQ effect depends on specific reward shaping. (Evidence: Sec 2.2; Appendix C Eq 13–14, Table 6)

Reproducibility

  • The provided paper summary does not specify whether code/data are publicly available.
  • Appendix C/D contains training hyperparameters, domain randomization ranges, reward functions, and fingertip fabrication dimensions/materials, providing a clear starting point for reproduction, but the seed/rollout counts/success rate aggregation methods for real-world experiments and code availability remain unconfirmed.
