SliceIt! - A Dual Simulator Framework for Learning Robot Food Slicing

SliceIt! is a real2sim2real framework that calibrates a high-fidelity cutting simulator with real slicing data, learns reinforcement-learning-based compliance control policies in a dual simulation environment, and deploys them to a real robot.

Cristian C. Beltran-Hernandez, Nicolas Erbetti, Masashi Hamaya

TL;DR

  • Learning a robot to cut food with a knife in the real world is dangerous and leads to significant food waste, so this work addresses the problem of safely learning in simulation.
  • We construct a dual-simulation environment connecting DiSECt (for cutting physics) and Gazebo (for robot dynamics) via ROS, calibrate it using a few real-world cutting data points, and then learn SAC-based reinforcement learning alongside FDCC compliance control.
  • In real-robot evaluation, the proposed method exhibited lower contact forces than the Gazebo-only baseline across all foods, including carrots which were not used during training.

Problem Definition

The task of cutting food requires precise movements that adapt to reaction forces generated by the material and the cutting board while handling a dangerous tool like a knife. Existing model-based control or learning-based methods may require extensive real-world interactions to adapt to varying physical properties of different foods, which can lead to unnecessary food waste in cutting tasks. Furthermore, having a robot hold a knife and perform random exploration is hazardous in the real world.

Learning in simulation allows for safe exploration, but the reality gap between simulation and the real world may prevent policies from functioning correctly on a real robot. Although high-fidelity cutting simulators like DiSECt have recently emerged, existing cutting simulators primarily focus on physical interactions between the knife and the object, without sufficiently considering whether a robot can actually realize those knife motions. This paper proposes a framework that combines a cutting simulator with a robot simulator to enable both real-robot evaluation and learning simultaneously.

Core Idea

SliceIt! employs a structure that utilizes two simulators with distinct strengths together. CutSim, based on DiSECt, models soft material cutting and contact forces more realistically, while RoboSim, based on Gazebo, is advantageous for robot dynamics and ROS integration. First, motion and contact force data are collected by cutting food a few times with a real robot, and this data is used to calibrate CutSim’s parameters. Subsequently, the RL agent learns not only the reference trajectory of the knife but also the control parameters of the compliance controller. When this learned policy is deployed on a real robot, it can perform cutting motions while reducing contact forces in response to changes in material stiffness and contact with the cutting board.

Methodology

System Overview

The overall workflow follows a real2sim2real structure. First, data from cutting food with a real robot is collected, and this data is used to calibrate the dual-simulation environment. Next, a control policy is learned using RL in the calibrated simulation environment and then deployed to the real robot. ROS serves as an intermediate layer connecting the two simulators, the real robot, the RL policy, and the low-level compliance controller.

CutSim: DiSECt Calibration

CutSim utilizes DiSECt. DiSECt represents soft material cutting by combining a signed distance field-based continuous contact model and a damage model with finite element method-based cutting simulation. Since this simulator is differentiable, gradient-based optimization can be used for parameter estimation.

The paper performs calibration in two stages. First, it quickly finds initial parameters using the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator via Optuna, rather than a gradient-based method. Subsequently, the Adam algorithm is used to fine-tune the parameters precisely. The goal is to reproduce the robot’s cutting motion and contact forces in simulation, obtaining force profiles similar to those in reality.

RoboSim: Gazebo and ROS Integration

RoboSim utilizes Gazebo. Gazebo was chosen due to its high compatibility with ROS and the ease of integrating robot system components. In this paper, Gazebo generates the knife motion for the simulated robot and exchanges information with CutSim via ROS messages.

Simulator Bridge

The reason for using two simulators together lies in the difference in computational cost and division of roles. CutSim requires a smaller time step; the paper reports that dt=1.0×10−5dt = 1.0 \times 10^{-5}dt=1.0×10−5 yielded the best results. RoboSim can operate with dt=1.0×10−4dt = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}dt=1.0×10−4, resulting in relatively lower computational cost.

The two simulators are run in parallel using an interleaving method where they execute alternating fixed time intervals. The actual control cycle is set to 2.0×10−32.0 \times 10^{-3}2.0×10−3 seconds, matching the maximum control frequency of the Universal Robots UR5e. RL-Based Cutting Learning

The cutting task is defined as an episodic Markov Decision Process with a finite time horizon TTT. It consists of a state space S\mathcal{S}S, action space A\mathcal{A}A, transition probability function P\mathcal{P}P, reward function R\mathfrak{R}R, and discount rate γ\gammaγ. The agent observes the state st\mathbf{s}_tst​, selects an action at\mathbf{a}_tat​ according to policy π\piπ, and receives the next state st+1\mathbf{s}_{t+1}st+1​ and reward rtr_trt​.

The learning algorithm used is Soft Actor-Critic (SAC). SAC is an off-policy actor-critic based maximum entropy reinforcement learning algorithm that optimizes both expected reward maximization and an entropy term that encourages exploration. It allows for the reuse of past experiences using a replay buffer, and the paper mentions that it can be extended with prioritized experience replay.

The RL agent’s observations include the knife position relative to the target location, velocity, jerkiness, previous actions, and a history of nnn force-torque readings obtained from sensors. The actor network combines a TCN (Temporal Convolutional Network) and a fully connected network. The TCN processes the force-torque history, while the fully connected network processes the remaining observations; their outputs are concatenated into 64-dimensional features before being passed to an additional fully connected network.

The agent’s action consists of the reference trajectory xc\mathbf{x}^cxc and control parameters [Kc,Kp,Kd][K^c, K^p, K^d][Kc,Kp,Kd] passed to FDCC. These values are provided at a lower control frequency, while FDCC generates joint commands at the robot’s maximum possible control frequency.

Compliance Control

Forward Dynamics Compliance Control (FDCC) is used for contact-rich food cutting tasks. FDCC combines impedance control, admittance control, and force control into a single Cartesian compliance strategy. It is described as having good stability even near singularities because it directly maps Cartesian inputs to joint control commands using the forward dynamics and forward kinematics of a virtual model.

The controller uses the Cartesian position x\mathbf{x}x of the robot end-effector, joint positions, measured contact force Fext\mathbf{F}_{ext}Fext​, target pose and wrench xd\mathbf{x}^dxd, Fd\mathbf{F}^dFd, and command wrench Fc\mathbf{F}^cFc. The forward dynamics and forward kinematics of the virtual model are expressed as H−1JTH^{-1}J^TH−1JT and FK(q)FK(q)FK(q), respectively. The controller is parameterized by stiffness KcK^cKc and PD gains KpK^pKp, KdK^dKd.

Experimental Results

The real robot platform consists of two Universal Robots UR5e arms. Each arm is equipped with a parallel gripper and a force-torque sensor. A custom finger adapter that allows attaching a kitchen knife is installed on the Robotiq 85 parallel gripper of the cutting arm. In the experiments, one arm fixes the food while the other performs a single-slice cutting motion.

Three types of food were used for calibration: tomato (representing low stiffness), cucumber (medium stiffness), and potato (high stiffness). During real data collection, the knife speed was kept constant. Four types of food—cucumber, tomato, potato, and carrot—were used for validation. Among these, carrots were not used in calibration and served to evaluate generalization performance.

The baseline involves simulating cutting motions and learning policies in a Gazebo-only environment. In the baseline, a compression spring is modeled as a prismatic joint, and the Error Reduction Parameter and Constraint Force Mixing parameters are adjusted. The adjustment goal is to ensure that the force required when the spring is compressed to its maximum matches the maximum force of the real force profile.

In both environments, the RL agent was trained for 80K time steps after calibration. During training, domain randomization was performed by loading one of the calibrated foods into CutSim, and small uniform distribution noise was injected into simulation parameters. Similar noise was applied to the baseline. The paper reports that learning converged at approximately 60K time steps.

In real robot evaluation, only one piece of each food type was used to minimize food waste. The experimental conditions are as follows:

Limitations

The primary limitation explicitly stated by the authors is the increase in computation time. Using the high-fidelity cutting simulator DiSECt requires more time for RL policy learning compared to a simple Gazebo-only environment. The paper reports that it takes approximately 40 hours when using the dual-simulation environment, whereas it takes about 4 hours with Gazebo alone. This difference is explained by the higher computational requirements of each simulation time step in DiSECt. While the authors believe the additional computation time is justified by improved real robot performance, they mention that researching ways to reduce computation time is valuable for future work.

Critical Review

Strengths

  • Practicality of the Dual-Simulator Configuration — It clearly addresses both cutting physics (via CutSim/DiSECt) and robot dynamics (via RoboSim/Gazebo/ROS) in parallel, considering not only cutting physics but also real robot executability, unlike existing simulators that focus solely on cutting physics (Evidence: Sec 1, Sec 3.B, Figs 1 & 2).
  • Contact Force Reduction vs. Baseline on Real Robot — Compared to the Gazebo-only baseline, lower actual cutting contact forces are reported for cucumber, tomato, potato, and carrot, with the same trend observed even for carrots which were not used in training (Evidence: Sec 4.B, Fig 7).
  • Learning FDCC Parameters via RL — The design where the RL agent outputs Kc,Kp,KdK^c,K^p,K^dKc,Kp,Kd for the compliance controller, in addition to robot motions, clearly aims to reduce the burden of manual tuning (Evidence: Sec 3.C.3, Fig 4).

Weaknesses & Questions

  • Simple Baseline and Limited Comparison — Since the baseline only matches maximum force using a compression spring in Gazebo, it is difficult to isolate whether the superiority of the proposed method stems from the dual-simulator effect or from more sophisticated calibration/RL tuning (Evidence: Sec 4.A.2, Figs 6 & 7) (Interpretation).
  • Lack of Statistical Evidence in Real Experiments — Only one piece of food per type was used, with a low number of slices (5–15), and there are no means, variances, success rates, or significance tests in the tables or text, leaving the claim of “consistently superior” performance weakly supported (Evidence: Table 2, Sec 4.B, Figs 7 & 8) (Interpretation).
  • Limited Generalization Scope — Validation is limited to single cross-section cuts, constant knife speed, and four similar vegetables. The claim of “unseen objects” relies solely on carrots, making it premature to extend conclusions to other shapes, hardnesses, or soft foods (Evidence: Sec 2.A, Sec 4.A.3, Table 2) (Interpretation).
  • Lack of Quantitative Validation for Calibration — Only the range of DiSECt parameters is presented; final values, error metrics against measured force profiles, and TPE+Adam iteration counts/convergence criteria are not reported, leaving the premise of “high-fidelity simulation” insufficiently verified (Evidence: Sec 3.B.1, Table 1, Fig 6).
  • Safety/Success Metrics Biased Toward Contact Force — Although the reward includes task completion/collision, actual evaluation primarily focuses on contact force curves. The absence of cutting success rates, thickness consistency, failure cases, or safety limit violations makes it difficult to draw “safe/accurate” conclusions (Evidence: Sec 3.C.4, Sec 4.B) (Interpretation).

Reproducibility

  • Although the text claims open-source availability, code repositories, real-world datasets, and final calibration parameters are not provided in the paper, hindering reproduction (Evidence: Sec 1, Table 1).
  • The SAC/TCN structure, reward weights w1,w2,w3w_1,w_2,w_3w1​,w2​,w3​, thresholds, domain randomization noise ranges, and computational environment are not specified, making it difficult to reproduce the 80K step training and the 40h vs. 4h results (Evidence: Sec 3.C, Sec 4.A.3, Sec 5).
