World Dreamer v5-lite: A Multi-Robot World Action Model with One Dreamer

World Dreamer v5-lite is a multi-robot world-action model that builds a shared workspace-coordinate world state from calibrated camera features, uses VLM-based per-robot queries to exchange pose-aware information, and predicts separate action chunks for each robot. Masked-view cooperative training encourages reliance on teammates' observations.

Guoning Wu, Zijian Cai, Yuhang Zhang, Ying Liu, Yongbin Zheng, arXiv, 2025

TL;DR

In multi-robot manipulation, camera observations from each robot are integrated into a shared world state ϕ\phiϕ in workspace coordinates, while per-robot action streams remain separate, exchanging information only through pose-aware queries. The VLM provides scene context and per-robot queries, video encoder features are loaded into ϕ\phiϕ using calibration geometry, and masked-view cooperative training teaches the model to utilize team observations even when its own camera is occluded. World Dreamer v5-lite achieves a mean success rate of 74.0% on RoboFactory and 78.8% on four real-robot tasks, outperforming the strongest evaluation baselines by 23.7%p and 13.8%p respectively, and records an overall score of 58.08% on RoboCasa365.

Problem Definition

Existing vision-language-action (VLA) models and world-action models primarily target single robots. In multi-robot manipulation, observations are distributed, and each robot’s camera moves while seeing only a portion of the workspace. Consequently, image patches from different cameras do not point to the same physical location.

Furthermore, control is decentralized, but decisions are coupled. Robots may have different embodiments and action spaces, yet one robot’s actions depend on the positions and movements of others. Observations can also be incomplete. If a robot’s camera is occluded by another robot or the object being manipulated, that robot must rely on information seen by its teammates’ cameras.

Simply concatenating camera tokens fails to establish correspondence in workspace coordinates. Using independent policies for each robot prevents leveraging team observations. Even if existing multi-agent policies possess information-sharing capabilities, the incentive to use other views is weak when one’s own camera provides sufficient information. This paper proposes a shared world state ϕ\phiϕ in common workspace coordinates and masked-view cooperative training to address these issues.

Core Idea

World Dreamer v5-lite aggregates calibrated video features from multiple robots into a fixed-size state ϕ\phiϕ in workspace coordinates. The VLM processes instructions and current images, with one learnable agent query placed for each robot slot. These queries share the same scene context while reading ϕ\phiϕ, exchanging information using relative pose data. The action streams themselves remain separate per robot, preserving heterogeneous embodiments and action spaces. During training, some camera views are masked simultaneously in both the VLM and video pathways, while all robots’ action targets are kept intact to induce cooperation that relies on team observations.

Video Encoder and Auxiliary Future Prediction

While the VLM provides semantic context from current images, the video encoder provides spatial evidence to construct ϕ\phiϕ. Recent RGB history is arranged into VVV camera slots. These camera slots are distinct from the NNN robot slots. A single robot may provide multiple views, or additional workspace cameras may be provided. A view mask mview∈{0,1}V\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{view}}\in\{0,1\}^Vmview∈{0,1}V selects available cameras, and the same mask determines which views of the current images enter the VLM.

A causal video variational autoencoder (VAE) converts each RGB history into latents, and the encoder of a pretrained video model processes To=3T_o=3To​=3 observed latent steps. During training, Tf=2T_f=2Tf​=2 noised future steps are additionally provided for an auxiliary video-flow prediction task. A prefix-asymmetric attention mask prevents observed tokens from attending to future steps. Consequently, observed outputs and ϕ\phiϕ depend only on observations, eliminating the need for video generation at inference time.

Action Decoder

The Action Decoder generates control for robots that may have different embodiments while considering inter-robot interactions. Concatenating action tokens from all robots into a single sequence entangles heterogeneous action spaces and increases sequence length as robots are added. Conversely, decoding each robot independently ignores other robots. Therefore, the paper separates per-robot action streams and allows inter-robot communication only by exchanging queries qi\mathbf{q}_iqi​.

The current proprioception of robot iii is si∈RDs\mathbf{s}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{D_s}si​∈RDs​, and the target action chunk is ai∈RH×Da\mathbf{a}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times D_a}ai​∈RH×Da​, where H=30H=30H=30 is the number of future control steps. Proprioception and actions are padded to common widths Ds=20D_s=20Ds​=20 and Da=18D_a=18Da​=18, allowing embodiments with different numbers of control coordinates to be processed by a single decoder. An action mask mact∈{0,1}N×H×Dc\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{act}}\in\{0,1\}^{N\times H\times D_c}mact∈{0,1}N×H×Dc​ indicates valid control coordinates and recorded future steps for each robot.

The interpolated chunk a~i\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_ia~i​ is projected into HHH action tokens Ai∈RH×d\mathbf{A}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times d}Ai​∈RH×d. Together with a learnable start embedding estart\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{start}}estart​, projected proprioception sˉi\bar{\mathbf{s}}_isˉi​, and query qi\mathbf{q}_iqi​, this forms the causal stream for robot iii:

[estart,sˉi,qi,Ai][\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{start}}, \bar{\mathbf{s}}_i, \mathbf{q}_i, \mathbf{A}_i][estart​,sˉi​,qi​,Ai​]

The decoder consists of L=36L=36L=36 layers, with each layer updating the stream in a read-decode-communicate order. This ensures that shared spatial evidence reaches the robot’s action tokens before its query is exchanged with other robots.

First, the Field Injection Block enables each query qi\mathbf{q}_iqi​ to read ϕ\phiϕ via cross-attention. Although all robots read the same state, attention is biased towards the camera positions assigned to robot iii, encouraging each robot to read ϕ\phiϕ from its own perspective without completely excluding distant areas.

Second, the Scene Decoder Block conditions robot iii‘s action stream on the scene-prefix cache. Each token causally attends only to the cache of the corresponding VLM layer and previous tokens in its own stream. Thus, action tokens can utilize language-image context, their own robot’s proprioception, field-updated queries, and previous action tokens while remaining separated from other robots’ action streams.

Third, the Agent Interaction Block exchanges all robots’ queries using pose-aware attention. The calibrated descriptor Pij∈R10\mathbf{P}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{10}Pij​∈R10 describes robot jjj‘s camera in robot iii‘s camera coordinate system, consisting of 3D translation, 6D continuous rotation, and Euclidean distance. A learned MLP creates the attention-logit bias bijh=fh(Pij)b_{ij}^h=f_h(\mathbf{P}_{ij})bijh​=fh​(Pij​) for head hhh. Since Pij\mathbf{P}_{ij}Pij​ is relative pose, it does not depend on the choice of world frame. The connection pattern is not fixed; instead, relative poses condition inter-robot exchange. Since only queries are exchanged, information from other robots reaches action tokens in subsequent layers, while each stream maintains its own proprioception and embodiment-specific action tokens.

The output head converts final action tokens into flow velocity u^i∈RH×Da\hat{\mathbf{u}}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times D_a}u^i​∈RH×Da​. At inference time, qi0\mathbf{q}_i^0qi0​, ϕ\phiϕ, and the scene-prefix cache are computed once per decision step, and the decoder is re-evaluated over five Euler steps to integrate velocity from noise to the action chunk.

Masked-View Cooperative Training

The above structure enables information sharing between robots but does not guarantee that this information is actually used. If all robots always see their own cameras, the decoder may rely primarily on its own view and ignore ϕ\phiϕ and other robots. Therefore, during training, a randomly selected robot’s entire camera or some views are masked using the view mask mview\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{view}}mview. The same cameras are removed simultaneously from both the VLM scene prefix and the ϕ\phiϕ construction pathway. Camera calibration continues to be used to define spatial references and relative pose descriptors Pij\mathbf{P}_{ij}Pij​.

Since view masking applies only to observations, recorded action chunks remain valid targets for all robots, including those with masked cameras. Remaining views can contribute through ϕ\phiϕ and the scene-prefix cache, and query interaction provides a context-sharing pathway.

In the training objective, each robot maintains the demonstrated action chunk as its target even under masked context. Flow time is sampled from ρ∼Beta(1.5,1)\rho\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1.5,1)ρ∼Beta(1.5,1), using τ=0.999(1−ρ)\tau=0.999(1-\rho)τ=0.999(1−ρ). This places more samples near the noisy starting point. The action loss Lact\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}}Lact​ is a weighted squared velocity error, first averaging supervised entries per robot and then averaging over all present robots.

Lact=E[1∣I∣∑i∈I1∣Ωi∣∑(h,c)∈Ωiωihc(u^ihc−uihc⋆)2]\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}} = \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|} \sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}} \frac{1}{|\Omega_i|} \sum_{(h,c)\in\Omega_i} \omega_{ihc}(\hat{u}_{ihc}-u_{ihc}^\star)^2 \right]Lact​=E​∣I∣1​i∈I∑​∣Ωi​∣1​(h,c)∈Ωi​∑​ωihc​(u^ihc​−uihc⋆​)2​

Here, the expectation is taken over scenes and flow time, and I\mathcal{I}I is the set of robots with supervised entries. Ωi\Omega_iΩi​ is the set of steps hhh and control coordinates ccc for robot iii selected by the action mask mact\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{act}}mact, and ωihc\omega_{ihc}ωihc​ is the per-entry weight. With probability 0.5, 1 to 6 demonstrated steps are provided as an action prefix, with supervision applied only to the remaining steps. In this case, ωihc\omega_{ihc}ωihc​ is the absolute action error from a detached rollout, normalized per robot and clipped to [0.5,5][0.5,5][0.5,5]. If there is no prefix, ωihc=1\omega_{ihc}=1ωihc​=1.

Two auxiliary losses train the video and state construction pathways. The generation tail predicts the noise-minus-latent velocity of future video latents, with Lfuture\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{future}}Lfuture​ being this prediction error. In Lfield\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{field}}Lfield​, detached observed tokens are passed through the state builder again. A rendering head samples from the 3D positions of the resulting state and predicts fixed random projections of normalized observed tokens. The loss combines cosine distance and Smooth-L1 error scaled by 0.1. Detachment restricts gradients to the builder and rendering head.

The total objective function is

λfield\lambda_{\mathrm{field}}λfield​ linearly decays from 0.1 to 0.02 during training. Masking alters visual inputs while preserving action targets, ensuring that remaining cameras provide evidence for all robots’ predictions.

Experimental Results

Evaluation targets include multi-robot collaboration on RoboFactory, single-robot household manipulation on RoboCasa365, and homogeneous and heterogeneous real-robot team tasks. The pretraining corpus combines manipulation trajectories from 20 simulated robot types, RoboFactory trajectories, and real-robot trajectories collected using LeKiwi and OpenArm. It includes single-arm and dual-arm configurations, over 60 task types, and more than 10,000 hours of interaction. Differences in action dimensions are handled by action masks, and missing cameras by view masks. The pretrained model is fine-tuned separately for each evaluation setting, and evaluation episodes are excluded from the training data.

Implementation Details

The vision-language backbone is initialized with Qwen3-VL. The vision encoder and language model are trained, while input embeddings and the language-modeling head are frozen. Training uses bfloat16 precision and DeepSpeed ZeRO Stage 2. One VLM forward pass is reused for four flow-matching samples in the action decoder.

Critical Review

Strengths

  • Shared world state structurally addresses multi-view correspondence — Instead of simply concatenating camera tokens, calibrated video features are integrated into a fixed-size state ϕ\phiϕ in workspace coordinates, providing a clear design where all robots read the same spatial reference regardless of the number of robots (Evidence: Section 3.3, Fig. 2).
  • Large reported performance gains on multi-robot benchmarks — RoboFactory mean success rate is 74.0%74.0\%74.0%, which is 23.723.723.7%p higher than the strongest baseline π0.5\pi_{0.5}π0.5​ (50.3%50.3\%50.3%), and the mean across four real-robot tasks is 78.8%78.8\%78.8%, exceeding π0.5\pi_{0.5}π0.5​‘s 65.0%65.0\%65.0% (Evidence: Table 1, Table 2).
  • Practical design avoiding future video generation at inference — Future video latents are used only for auxiliary training purposes; at inference, actions are predicted using only observed features and the vision-language model’s (VLM) scene prefix (key/value, KV) cache, reducing the burden of test-time video generation (Evidence: Section 3.2, Section 3.4).

Weaknesses & Questions

  • Lack of experiments separating contribution from effect — The plane layout of ϕ\phiϕ is similar to PDFactor/EG3D, and multi-agent cooperation has been addressed by GauDP/CLS-DP. However, there are no ablation studies showing which of ϕ\phiϕ, masked-view training, or pose-aware query exchange drives performance, leaving the claim of “shared world state and cooperative learning” unverified (Evidence: Section 2, Sections 3.3–3.5; no ablation tables/sections in text).
  • Target effect of masked-view training not verified at test time — Although the design aims to hide some cameras to force reliance on other robots’ observations, there are no success rates or occlusion robustness results under conditions where cameras are missing during evaluation. Thus, the conclusion of “cooperation under incomplete observation” is not directly supported (Evidence: Section 3.5; no such evaluation in experiments section).
  • Statistical uncertainty not reported — Table 1, Table 2, and Fig. 4 present only mean success rates without repeated runs, standard deviation/confidence intervals, or significance tests. Differences of a few %p at the scale of 100100100 trials/envs require careful interpretation (Evidence: Section 4.1, Table 1, Table 2, Fig. 4).
  • Weak direct link between RoboCasa365 comparison and multi-robot claims — RoboCasa365 involves single-robot household manipulation, and baseline results are taken from the official leaderboard. Therefore, superiority on this benchmark may be evidence of general manipulation/learning scaling rather than multi-robot cooperation capability (Evidence: Section 4.1, Section 4.2, Fig. 4; interpretation).
  • Narrow scope of real-world generalization — Real-robot evaluation is limited to four tasks with LeKiwi and OpenArm teams, and RoboFactory is limited to 2–4 agent tasks. Generalization to more robots, heterogeneous embodiments, or different work environments is difficult to confirm with current experiments alone (Evidence: Section 4.1, Table 1, Table 2).
  • Cooperation capability not separated from model/data differences in baseline comparison — Sharing a single-robot policy across multiple robots likely limits cross-agent information pathways, and there are no variants removing only the cooperation pathway from the same architecture, making it difficult to attribute performance differences solely to the cooperation mechanism (Evidence: Section 4.2; interpretation).
